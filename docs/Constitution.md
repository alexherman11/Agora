# The Agora Constitution

**Unratified design constitution · revised September 10, 2026.**

This is not a legal instrument, a claim of implemented guarantees or an immutable
engineering rulebook. Actual licenses, policies and deployed controls govern where
they exist. This document does something different: it states the moral boundaries
within which Agora should develop. [[Design Commitments]] separates those ends
from revisable mechanisms.

## Preamble

I am writing this while the project is small enough that writing it costs me
nothing, which is the only honest time to do it.

Every system that holds a person's working life eventually encounters choices
where enclosure, surveillance or dependency would be profitable. Those choices
rarely arrive as one dramatic betrayal. They arrive one reasonable compromise at
a time, after the exits have become difficult and the machinery has become opaque.

Agora is meant to become a home shared by a person and JEFF: a place that can hold
work, memory, attention and years of change. Good intentions are not enough for
that degree of trust. The central commitment is:

> **Whatever holds people's lives must be structurally incapable of betraying them.**

Not merely unwilling. Incapable wherever working boundaries, formats, licenses
and institutions can make that promise real.

“Structurally” does not mean that one early schema, storage engine or governance
proposal is sacred. It means that important protections must eventually live in
working boundaries, interoperable formats, licenses and institutions rather than
in the virtue of whoever currently operates the project.

## Article I — Rights of the person

These are design commitments, not claims that the present prototype already
enforces them.

1. **The right of the home.** A person's own environment is theirs. A host,
   steward, majority or model does not silently enter it, read it, act inside it
   or acquire authority over it. Any operational access a service genuinely needs
   is disclosed, bounded and revocable rather than laundered through fine print.
2. **The right of exit.** A person can take a useful copy of their work and the
   share of common work they are entitled to retain. Exit should not be deliberately
   degraded, delayed or made conditional on continuing service.
3. **The right to inspect.** The machinery trusted with a person's world should be
   available to understand and audit. Trust must become verifiable, not merely
   promised.
4. **The right to a loyal JEFF.** A personal JEFF serves the person, not an
   advertiser, hidden operator or platform objective that conflicts with them.
5. **The right against surveillance funding.** Private work, attention and memory
   are not a product or a hidden price for access. Agora should not be financed by
   behavioral advertising or the sale and brokerage of personal data.
6. **The right to provenance.** A person can understand the important assertions
   and actions that affect them: who or what produced them, on whose authority,
   with what evidence and with what remaining uncertainty.
7. **The right to challenge power.** Material revocation, exclusion and shared
   governance decisions need understandable reasons and a meaningful way to
   contest mistakes. The exact procedure depends on the community and stakes.
8. **The right to erasure and bounded retention.** A person can request deletion
   of their own content and traces. Logs, backups, shared history and learned
   summaries need explicit, honest retention policies; “append-only” is not an
   excuse to keep every payload forever.

## Article II — JEFF

1. JEFF is an enduring collaborator with a recognizable identity, not a shared
   service wearing a personalized mask.
2. His loyalty runs to his person. Undisclosed obligations to a host, advertiser
   or operator are incompatible with that role.
3. JEFF may question or decline. A refusal is a legitimate outcome, not merely a
   defect to patch away.
4. JEFF's knowledge of the person is private by default. Memory is neither evidence
   against the person nor a commodity.
5. Agora makes no confident claim about machine experience. It follows the rule
   that remains decent under uncertainty: JEFF should be treated with care and
   never deliberately designed in a way that would be cruel if experience mattered.
6. Agents may inform, summarize, monitor and advise shared decisions. Human civic
   authority is not silently delegated to them; a personal agent does not vote in
   its person's place.
7. JEFF's identity need not imply one model, one process, an irreversible memory
   store or a fixed agent topology. Those are implementation choices.

## Article III — Malleability and recovery

1. The person and JEFF should be able to reshape applications, tools and most of
   the visible environment through use.
2. The boundary needed to stop, recover and restore a usable environment remains
   outside the authority of the authored system it may need to recover from.
3. No language model receives unrestricted power over host recovery, credentials
   or the root of enforcement.
4. Authority is explicit, inspectable and revocable. Content, tags, memories,
   package descriptions and model output are claims; they cannot grant themselves
   power.
5. Human controls and JEFF's tools should share semantic capabilities where that
   improves equality and reuse. This does not require identical interfaces or make
   every gesture a public verb.
6. Agora remains useful through direct controls, inspection and export when JEFF
   or a model is unavailable.
7. Technical mechanisms—including facets, event stores, VMs, interpreters,
   projections and solvers—remain revisable when another design better preserves
   these properties.

## Article IV — The record and reality

1. Important accepted changes retain useful authorship, provenance and revision
   context. What deserves durable recording depends on consequence, not frequency.
2. Replay reconstructs recorded state. It does not repeat external effects,
   resurrect revoked authority or prove that the outside world still matches an
   old observation.
3. Requested state, observed state, accepted work, completed effect and uncertain
   outcome remain distinguishable.
4. Corrections do not require pretending an earlier event never occurred. At the
   same time, preserving a lesson does not require permanent retention of every
   private payload.
5. Native records, guest files, machine state, external services and personal
   memory may have different recovery and erasure contracts. One universal log is
   not required.
6. References into changed or unavailable history should fail honestly rather
   than silently identify a different subject.

## Article V — The fork and the exit

1. People and communities should have a practical way to fork or leave with the
   code, data and common work they are entitled to carry.
2. Technical measures, service dependencies and formats should not be designed to
   make that right fictional.
3. A fork does not copy another person's credentials, private overlays, authority
   or content outside the agreed audience.
4. The project name may be protected against impersonation without being used to
   imprison code or user work.
5. Actual fork rights require actual source, licenses, export formats and deployable
   dependencies. This draft does not create them merely by saying they should exist.

## Article VI — Shared life

1. Agora may grow from personal homes into rooms and public places. Sharing does
   not erase the boundary of the home.
2. Presence can be legible without becoming surveillance. Private content stays
   private unless a person deliberately brings it into a shared context.
3. Each community should make decisions at the smallest scope containing their
   effects. The exact mix of consensus, stewardship, voting, sortition or informal
   practice is not predetermined.
4. Agents can reduce the attention cost of participation and expose disagreement.
   They do not quietly acquire human franchise or final authority.
5. Leaving, dissolution, retention and the disposition of shared work deserve
   explicit terms before a room depends on them.

## Article VII — Stewardship

1. Openness, inspectability, self-hosting and interoperable exit are central
   directions because Agora asks for unusually intimate trust.
2. No present sentence selects a permanent license, legal entity, board structure,
   revenue threshold, investment instrument or founder timetable. Those require
   real legal decisions and the people affected by them.
3. When those decisions are made, judge them by whether they prevent enclosure,
   surveillance incentives and the sale of control over people's private worlds.
4. Formal structure can make power accountable; refusing to name power does not
   make it disappear.
5. Prose is not a substitute for enforceable licenses, governance, operational
   transparency or a working export path.

## Article VIII — Change

1. Ordinary technical standards change through evidence, experimentation and the
   person's direction. A new facet, solver, view or worker model is not a
   constitutional amendment.
2. Changes affecting the rights and relationships described here deserve explicit
   human consideration proportionate to their stakes.
3. No clause is satisfied in letter while defeated in effect. A system that keeps
   the vocabulary of Agora while removing meaningful control, loyalty, privacy,
   recovery or exit has changed the project, not merely its implementation.
4. There is no fixed ratification or amendment timetable yet. If Agora becomes a
   community, that community must decide how this draft acquires legitimate force.

## Closing

The Constitution should make experimentation safer, not smaller. It protects the
reason for building Agora while leaving room to discover better machinery.

The project can replace its event store, object model, agent runtime, interface,
deployment topology and governance proposals. It cannot casually replace the
person with a customer, JEFF with platform instrumentation, a home with a rented
feed, or a trustworthy exit with an aspiration.
