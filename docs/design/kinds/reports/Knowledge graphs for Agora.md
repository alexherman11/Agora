# Agora's log already contains its knowledge graph

Agora does not need to adopt a knowledge-graph product. Its append-only record log already *is* a provenance graph: who acted, for whom, which verb, which things, what the actor saw and what caused it. That maps almost one to one onto the W3C PROV vocabulary, and Datomic and XTDB show what "as of" queries over such a log are good for. The work ahead is to build readings on top of the log, not a new store. Those readings are backlinks, history, "explain", neighbourhoods, paths, facets and seeded random walks. Eighty years of history say which parts to copy and which to avoid. The parts that lasted gave the author a payoff the same day: schema.org, backlinks, Wikidata's claim-plus-reference statements, OSM's free tags with editor presets. The parts that failed asked for an ontology or infrastructure first and delivered value later or to someone else: the Semantic Web's reasoning agents, NEPOMUK's desktop triple store, and `owl:sameAs`, an identity link so strong that one careless assertion spreads every property across two entities. For AI agents the evidence is narrower than the hype. Graph memory beats vector retrieval on multi-hop, temporal and "what is still true" questions. It loses or ties on simple fact lookup. On today's chat benchmarks, plain full-context and filesystem baselines come close to it. Agora has one advantage the literature never tests: its edges are *authored* by people and agents rather than extracted by an LLM, and extraction is where most of the cost and noise in published graph systems comes from. For a 200-neighbour community map, the hard problems are governance and location privacy, not inference. The mechanism that recurs across every successful system is the same one: **every machine contribution is a proposal carrying its reason, written into the log, reversible, and accepted by a person or the owning agent.**

## Eighty years of graphs reward a payoff today, not an ontology tomorrow

Vannevar Bush's 1945 memex is closer to Agora than most of what followed. It was "a desk" holding one person's "books, records, and communications." Bush rejected filing in which an item "can be in only one place" and is found "by tracing it down from subclass to subclass." He named the essential feature: **"The process of tying two items together is the important thing."** ([Bush 1945](https://web.mit.edu/STS.035/www/PDFs/think.pdf)). His links were named, written on *both* items, and gathered into **trails**: ordered, annotatable paths with side branches that a user could photograph and pass "to his friend for insertion in his own memex, there to be linked into the more general trail." He also kept the conventional code index beside association rather than replacing it ([Bush 1945](https://web.mit.edu/STS.035/www/PDFs/think.pdf)). Most descendants kept the bare link and dropped the trail. In Agora a trail is naturally a thing in its own right, with a name, an owner, an order and comments (imagined). A backlink is a derived reading of a link fact in the log, never a second write that can drift.

The Semantic Web is the cautionary tale of ambition. By 2006 its founders said the vision of agents reasoning over shared ontologies "remains largely unrealized" ([Shadbolt, Berners-Lee & Hall 2006](https://dl.acm.org/citation.cfm?id=1155373)). What survived was a thin layer of shared vocabularies and serialisations: a 2014 crawl found schema.org on **more than 700,000 pay-level domains**, and JSON-LD spread widely ([Hitzler, Janowicz & Hogan 2020](https://doi.org/10.3233/SW-190387)). The survivors share one property: the publisher got something back right away, such as richer search results or a drop-in format. The failures asked publishers to pay up front for value that arrived later and went to someone else (imagined synthesis). The identity layer failed in its own way. `owl:sameAs` encodes one point on a similarity scale that is "often too strong for many of its current uses," and in practice it was asserted far more strongly than its authors meant ([Halpin et al., ISWC 2010](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-642-17746-0_20.pdf)). A secondary slide deck reports that bad sameAs links between DBpedia and DBLP attached publications to the wrong authors ([KG identity slides](https://www.lri.fr/~sais/KGC/4-IdentityProblem.pdf)).

Freebase showed that a community-editable typed graph could scale. By January 2014 it held **about 44 million topics and 2.4 billion facts**. Each topic carried *one or more types*. Users could create types but not edit other people's, and a staff member promoted user types into the commons ([Wikipedia: Freebase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freebase_(database))). Google bought it, and launched its Knowledge Graph in 2012 with **more than 500 million objects and 3.5 billion facts** under the slogan "things, not strings" ([Google blog](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/search/introducing-knowledge-graph-things-not/)). The value came from disambiguated identity plus typed facts, not from inference. Freebase then shut down in 2016 ([Wikipedia: Freebase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freebase_(database))). Its data was moved into Wikidata through a human review queue, the Primary Sources Tool, and the migrators reported that "the cultural difference between the two involved communities was more demanding than the structural challenges" ([Pellissier Tanon et al., WWW 2016](https://research.google/pubs/from-freebase-to-wikidata-the-great-migration/)). Two lessons follow for Agora. One is custody: a community graph must outlive its host, so the log and the dictionary have to export as plain text. The other is that norms such as "claims about people need a source" have to be written into the world, not assumed (imagined).

Wikidata holds the most mature answer to "many authors, including bots, asserting facts about the same thing." Each statement is a claim (property, value, qualifiers such as time) plus references and a rank. It allows the special values "no value" and "unknown value." Ranks are *preferred*, *normal* and *deprecated*, and conflicting statements coexist. Its own primer says the system is "not about the truth, but about statements and their references" ([Wikibase DataModel Primer](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Wikibase/DataModel/Primer)). That stance suits a world where a person's claim and an agent's inference must sit side by side. Its best-documented failure is a warning for Agora's kinds. Editors constantly confuse instance-of (P31) with subclass-of (P279): **about 444,000 of 935,000 former P279 edges were replaced by P31** ([Shenoy et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.00156)). A 2016 study flagged **87.5% of 17,819 classes** in multi-level taxonomies for classification problems ([Dadalto et al.](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/content/evidence-large-scale-conceptual-disarray-multi-level-taxonomies-wikidata-0)), and the confusion persists in the October 2024 dump ([arXiv 2511.04926](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04926v2)). A class/instance distinction that is mandatory but means nothing to most editors produces this kind of disorder. Agora should not make people or agents choose between "is a" and "is a kind of" when they write.

The semantic desktop projects of the 2000s tried exactly what Agora is trying, and the best-documented one failed on cost without felt value. In KDE, NEPOMUK "was not delivering the performance and user experience" expected and was replaced by Baloo in KDE SC 4.13 ([Wikipedia: NEPOMUK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEPOMUK_(software))). Initial indexing hogged CPU and RAM, and RDF proved oversized for the desktop, so Baloo kept indexing and dropped RDF ([Xmodulo](https://www.xmodulo.com/kde-semantic-desktop-nepomuk-baloo.html)). In a two-month Gnowsis study with **8 participants, only 2 kept using the prototype**, and simple *has-part* and *is-related* relations were enough to file and re-find information ([Sauermann & Heim](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-540-88564-1_30.pdf)). Lifestreams' time-ordered stream with filters is the idea from that era that most survived ([Lifestreams project](http://cs-www.cs.yale.edu/homes/freeman/lifestreams.html)). Agora's log already is a lifestream, and its lenses are Lifestreams' filters. The NEPOMUK lesson is about ingestion. Crawling the whole disk into a graph is the trap. The things people and agents *make or touch* are a smaller, higher-value set, and outside sources should come in through adapters on demand, with an off switch (imagined).

Personal knowledge tools rediscovered Bush's two-ended link. User reports converge, though only anecdotally: **backlinks and the local graph are useful, while the global graph view is mostly decorative**, its positions unstable on every load and its picture crowded and unreadable ([Obsidian forum](https://forum.obsidian.md/t/whats-the-point-of-the-graph-view-how-are-you-using-it/71316?page=2)). These tools succeeded where semantic desktops failed because a `[[link]]` costs nothing in the flow of writing and pays back at once, the same principle as schema.org.

## Verbs bound to aspects let every actor act through one path

The systems that make a graph *operational* treat verbs as first-class schema. Palantir Foundry splits its ontology into a "semantic" half (objects, properties, links) and a "kinetic" half (actions, functions, dynamic security) ([Palantir: Ontology overview](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/ontology/overview/)). An action type is "a single transaction that changes the properties of one or more objects," carrying validation, permissions and side effects such as notifications and webhooks. Defining it once means "the same action logic and validations [are] made available across all user-facing applications" ([Palantir: Action types](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/action-types/overview/)). **Interface rules** apply an action to any object type that implements an interface, which binds verbs to a shape rather than a concrete type ([Palantir: Action rules](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/action-types/rules/)). AIP agents never get raw write access. They are handed a configured subset of action and query tools, and each action tool "can be configured to run automatically or to run after confirmation from the user" ([Palantir: Agent Studio tools](https://www.palantir.com/docs/foundry/agent-studio/tools/)). Agora could copy this directly. Its verbs already live in a world dictionary. What it lacks is a **per-agent verb grant with a confirm-or-auto flag**, so that people and every class of agent act through the same verbs with the same checks (imagined).

Tana shows the consumer version. Commands attached to a supertag "show as buttons on all tagged nodes" and can fire on events such as "when added" or "when checked." A node can carry several supertags, and "extend" projects a parent's fields into a child that may add fields and change defaults but cannot delete what it inherited ([Tana docs: Supertags](https://outliner.tana.inc/learn/features/supertags)). The same page carries a warning: **tags changed through the API do not fire these events.** If agents act through a different path than people, type-level triggers diverge. Tana also needs 13 fixed "base types" (Meeting, Task, Person) so that AI features know what a user-named tag means. Agora will likely need a similar mapping from world-defined kinds to a small set of well-known kinds (imagined).

The Windows shell is an old, battle-tested verb dictionary keyed by type plus predicate. It uses canonical, language-independent verb names separate from display labels. It has visibility tiers: normal verbs, "extended" verbs shown only on Shift+right-click, and `ProgrammaticAccessOnly` verbs never shown in a menu, which is a natural home for agent-only verbs. `AppliesTo` conditions over item properties decide applicability, and `MultiSelectModel` hides a verb when the selection count doesn't fit ([Microsoft Learn: Shortcut menu handlers](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/shell/context-menu-handlers)). Entity–component–system design gives the cleanest general answer to "which verbs apply here?". A system acts "globally over all entities that have the required components," so applicability is a *query over aspects present*, not a lookup on a class ([Wikipedia: ECS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entity_component_system)). Prototype delegation adds live, editable kinds, since changing a shared prototype changes all existing instances ([MDN: prototype chain](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Inheritance_and_the_prototype_chain)). Put together, these suggest a model for Agora (imagined). **Aspects are components. A verb is a system keyed by required aspects. A kind is a named prototype: a default bundle of aspects and verbs that an object delegates to and may locally shadow.** The cost, which ECS names, is that behaviour becomes non-local, so each verb on an object needs provenance ("this verb comes from kind X via aspect Y").

Kinds should *describe and check* rather than forbid. The W3C split between OWL and SHACL helps here. OWL derives facts from incomplete data under an open-world assumption, while SHACL validates data against shapes under a closed-world one ([Ahmetaj et al.](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2108.06096v2)), which matches what users expect a "kind" to do ([TopQuadrant](https://archive.topquadrant.com/?p=7325)). An Agora kind can behave like an advisory SHACL shape over open aspects: it reports "this event has no time" without refusing extra aspects (imagined). Kinds can also emerge from use. Formal concept analysis over each entity's *property set* builds a lattice of candidate types, and it has been applied to successive Wikidata versions ([TU Dresden FCA](https://iccl.inf.tu-dresden.de/web/Inproceedings3195)). Because the number of concepts can grow exponentially ([TU Dresden](https://iccl.inf.tu-dresden.de/web/Modelling_Dynamics_in_Semantic_Web_Knowledge_Graphs_with_Formal_Concept_Analysis)), the practical form is an iceberg lattice of frequent key sets. Agora can surface it as "38 things share `when`, `where`, `who` and have no kind; make an *event* kind?", for a person to name and accept (imagined).

The failure that recurs at every scale is **lock-in of links and verbs**. A competitor argues that data inside Palantir lives "in a proprietary shape and format" whose exports other systems cannot readily use ([HASH, vendor-authored](https://hash.ai/blog/the-problem-with-palantir)). Commentators say production instances take months to over a year to stand up ([Xpert.Digital](https://xpert.digital/en/why-managed-ai-is-the-better-choice/?amp=1)), and frame the real test as whether customers keep "ontologies, access controls, action logs... current" ([BTW Media](https://btw.media/en/palantirs-real-ai-test-is-whether-the-ontology-still-holds-months-later)). Notion shows the same flattening on a small scale: relations export to CSV as plain URLs that cannot be re-imported ([Notion help](https://www.notion.com/help/relations-and-rollups)). None of this criticism is independent measurement, but the direction is consistent. Agora's kinds, links and verbs should export as plain text in the same notation people and agents use, so the graph never lives only inside the engine.

## The log is a provenance graph, and "as of" turns it into accountable memory

W3C PROV defines three node types: Entity, Activity and Agent. Its core edges include `used`, `wasGeneratedBy`, `wasDerivedFrom`/`wasRevisionOf`, `wasAssociatedWith`, `wasInformedBy`, and `actedOnBehalfOf`, the last recording "accountability of an agent towards another agent" ([W3C PROV-O](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o/)). Agora's record maps onto it with no new data (imagined mapping):

| Agora record field | PROV term |
|---|---|
| the record | Activity |
| who acted | `wasAssociatedWith` → Agent |
| for whom | `actedOnBehalfOf` (actor → principal) |
| verb | `hadPlan` / activity type |
| what the actor saw | `used` |
| fact `[thing, aspect, hash before, value]` | new Entity version, `wasGeneratedBy` the record, `wasRevisionOf` the version named by the hash |
| what caused it | `wasInformedBy` (record → record) |

PROV adds two ideas Agora can use. `specializationOf` separates "note a123 as of hash H" from "note a123": people's traversals want the general thing, while audit wants the specific version. A `prov:Bundle` is "a named set of provenance descriptions, and is itself an Entity" ([W3C PROV-O](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-o/)). That suggests a way for agents to annotate the log ("I think record X was a mistake") with records about records, without editing anything (imagined).

Datomic shows what an accretive log buys. `as-of` returns the database at a past point, `since` returns only what was added after a point, and `history` returns "the present and the unfiltered past, including retractions". Transactions are entities that can carry metadata, and the docs filter out facts from transactions with low `:source/confidence` ([Datomic filters](https://docs.datomic.com/reference/filters.html)). Agora's fact is a datom with a stronger guard. "Hash before" makes every write a compare-and-set and gives each fact its own `wasRevisionOf` edge. Fowler's event-sourcing essay lists what such systems must otherwise add later: recorded responses from external queries, so replay is deterministic, and stored prior values, so changes can be reversed ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/EventSourcing.html)). Agora already records both: "what the actor saw" is the recorded input to an AI decision. In practice that means opening the world as of a record's predecessor answers "what did this agent see when it did that?" Undo becomes a new record that writes the old value forward. And "since my last record" is the change feed that should wake a resident (imagined).

On time, XTDB separates **system time**, when information entered the system, from **valid time**, when a fact holds in the world, and queries across both with interval operators ([XTDB key concepts](https://docs.xtdb.com/concepts/key-concepts.html)). Fowler warns that bitemporal handling gets "very messy" and should be avoided unless needed ([Fowler](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/EventSourcing.html)). The right split for Agora is to keep log order as the only *kernel* time axis and treat valid time as an ordinary aspect where a kind needs it, such as a mail sent yesterday and pulled in today (imagined). For deletion, Datomic keeps two operations apart. Retraction is the normal correction. **Excision** is a rare, irreversible removal for privacy or retention that "is not a way to fix errors," leaves a permanent record of itself, and should stay within a few thousand datoms because of indexing load ([Datomic excision](https://docs.datomic.com/operation/excision.html)). That is the model Agora's clutter design and community privacy need: supersede by default, and excise loudly and rarely.

Lineage tooling adds impact analysis. OpenLineage reduces lineage to Job, Run and Dataset plus extensible "facets," including a `parent` run facet ([OpenLineage object model](https://openlineage.io/docs/spec/object-model)). Practitioners say column-level lineage is what makes "what breaks if I change this" precise ([Galaxy glossary](https://www.getgalaxy.io/learn/glossary/column-level-lineage-open-source-tools-explained)). Agora has both halves (imagined). A lens, rule or verb *declares* which aspects it reads, and the log *records* what each act actually saw and wrote. Comparing the two finds dependencies that were declared but never used, and dependencies that were used but never declared. Because Agora's facts are already aspect-level, renaming an aspect can be checked exactly.

Security research supplies both the strongest use of this graph and its worst failure mode. System-provenance intrusion detection suffers **dependency explosion**: coarse edges make everything look connected, and fine-grained capture is too costly to deploy ([provenance-graph survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.01722)). The 2025–26 work on LLM agents converges on one mechanism. Every value is labelled with where it came from and who may read it. Untrusted content is kept out of the planner by reference. Labels are checked deterministically at consequential calls. CaMeL solved **77% of AgentDojo tasks with provable security (84% undefended) and let 0 of 949 attacks succeed**, at about **2.8× the tokens** ([CaMeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18813)). FIDES cut successful injections from 156 to 23 without policies, but lost **up to 24.5% utility**, and its label creep is the agent version of dependency explosion ([FIDES](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23643v2)). RTBAS screens which inputs a step actually depended on and reports blocking all targeted attacks at about 2% utility loss ([RTBAS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08966)). The Agentic Reference Monitor adds a subtler case: an attacker can learn from a *denial*, so denied actions belong in the provenance graph too ([ARM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04035)).

Agora's record already holds the inputs these defences need (imagined). "Saw" is the `used` set, "for whom" names the principal, and "caused by" gives control dependence. So a fact's integrity label can be *computed* as the join over what its record saw, instead of being stored, and a policy change then applies to old facts without rewriting them. The minimal policy follows FIDES's P-T rule. Consequential verbs (send, delete, change a standing rule, run code on the PC) need a record whose "saw" set has no untrusted ancestor, such as adapter mail or stream chat. Otherwise they escalate to Alex as a one-click confirmation. To avoid dependency explosion, where the charter or the board becomes an ancestor of everything, Agora should record which *aspects* were read rather than whole things. It should also keep two sets on each record: a broad **saw** set for security and a narrow **cited** set for explanation. An "explain" verb then walks from a fact to the record that wrote it, opens what that record saw *as of* that moment, and follows "caused by" up to a human request. That explanation comes from the log, not from the agent's after-the-fact story, which is less reliable. One limit applies to every scheme here: none stops plain text-to-text persuasion ([CaMeL](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18813)).

## Graph memory helps agents with change and connection, not plain recall

The headline systems are real, but their wins are narrower than they look. Microsoft's GraphRAG extracts an entity graph with an LLM, clusters it, pre-summarises each community, and answers corpus-wide questions by map-reduce over those summaries. On two corpora of about 1M tokens it beat vector RAG on comprehensiveness **72–83%** of the time, and its root-level summaries used **more than 97% fewer tokens** than summarising the source text, though vector RAG won *directness* in every comparison ([Edge et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.16130v2)). Indexing is the cost. Microsoft's own LazyGraphRAG cuts indexing to **0.1%** of GraphRAG's by skipping up-front summarisation, and matches its global-search quality at **more than 700× lower query cost** ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag/)). Independent work is more sobering. Han et al. found RAG better on single-hop and detail questions and graph methods better on multi-hop. On MultiHop-RAG, plain RAG scored 67.02, community-GraphRAG local 69.01 and HippoRAG2 70.27, while triplet-only KG retrieval scored only 41.24. On temporal queries the graph method nearly doubled RAG, **50.60 vs 30.70** ([Han et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11371v1)). Zeng et al. showed that the usual LLM-judge evaluations are fragile: swapping answer order shifted win rates by more than 30%, a 25-token length difference moved them by more than 50%, and under an unbiased setup the gains were "much more moderate than reported" ([Zeng et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06331)). GraphRAG-Bench starts from the observation that GraphRAG "frequently underperforms vanilla RAG on many real-world tasks" ([Xiang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05690v3)).

The most useful mechanism for Agora is personalised PageRank. HippoRAG seeds a PageRank walk from the entities in a query over an LLM-extracted graph. It improved multi-hop QA by **up to 20%**, and its single-step retrieval matched iterative retrieval while being **10–30× cheaper and 6–13× faster** ([Gutiérrez et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14831)). Its successor admits that structure-augmented retrieval did *worse* than standard RAG on basic factual memory until passages were integrated as nodes ([HippoRAG 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14802)). Spreading activation from cognitive science (Collins & Loftus) and ACT-R's recency-and-frequency decay belong to the same family, and are being re-imported into agent memory with lateral inhibition and decay ([SYNAPSE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.02744); [ACT-R decay notes](https://dev.to/futhgar/eviction-without-deletion-running-an-act-r-decay-policy-for-agent-memory-36hi)). The Agora version is a single "activation" reading (imagined). It is seeded by what is on screen and who is speaking, runs over the edges people and agents already authored, and is decayed by *event count* rather than wall-clock time, because JEFF sleeps between calls. Agora can skip HippoRAG's most error-prone step, LLM triple extraction, entirely.

Zep's Graphiti is the published design closest to Agora. It keeps a non-lossy **episode** layer of raw messages, a derived **entity/fact** layer and a **community** layer. Its edges are bitemporal. When a new fact contradicts an old one, the old edge is invalidated, not deleted. Graph writes go through *predefined* Cypher, never LLM-written queries ([Rasmussen et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13956v1)). On LongMemEval it beat full-context by 15–18.5% (gpt-4o: **71.2% vs 60.2%**) with about **90% lower latency** and roughly 1.6k instead of 115k tokens. It lost badly on questions about what the *assistant itself* said (gpt-4o: 94.6% → 80.4%) ([Zep](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.13956v1)). For Agora that means agents' own outputs have to be first-class episodes. Elsewhere, simple baselines stay strong. Mem0's graph variant adds only **about 2%** over its plain fact memory ([Chhikara et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19413)). Letta reports that an agent which simply stores history in files and searches them scores **74.0% on LoCoMo**, above specialised memory libraries ([Letta research](https://letta-home-site.pages.dev/research/)). A secondary audit says LoCoMo's own answer key is 6.4% wrong ([braindetox](https://braindetox.kr/en/posts/memory_benchmark_scores_reliability_2026.html)). LongMemEval names the abilities that actually separate systems: multi-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, **knowledge updates** and abstention. Commercial assistants lose about 30% accuracy over sustained interactions, and time-aware query expansion helps ([Wu et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10813)). These are JEFF's questions after a context reset: "what changed since I last looked, and what is still true?"

How agents *use* a graph matters as much as having one. Think-on-Graph lets the LLM walk entities and relations step by step with beam search. It needs no training, its paths can be traced and corrected, and it set the best result on 6 of 9 datasets ([Sun et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07697)). Asking a model to write full graph queries remains brittle, reaching **up to 61.58% execution accuracy** on CypherBench, and even that only with domain-specific property-graph views ([CypherBench](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2412.12612)). In Agora's small text language, the walk pattern ("show this thing's neighbours; pick; go") fits better: each step is a checkable command, and the path is a record Alex can read afterwards (imagined). Coordination results point the same way. Salemi et al. found that a blackboard, where a coordinator posts requests and sub-agents decide for themselves whether to answer, gave **13–57% relative gains** in end-to-end success over the best baseline ([Salemi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01285v2)). Collaborative Memory attaches immutable provenance to every memory fragment and gives each agent a permission-filtered read view ([Collaborative Memory, ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18279v1)). Agora's world and Board already form a blackboard, and the log already supplies immutable provenance. What is missing is a read-policy word that filters the graph per agent and per person (imagined).

Taken together, the evidence favours a layered retriever (imagined). It starts with exact object lookup and recency over the log, which is cheap and strong. Dense similarity comes next. Seeded graph diffusion follows, for "what is connected." Last comes a validity filter for "is this still true." The graph earns its keep mainly in the last two layers. No published benchmark tests memory for an agent working in a shared, multi-writer object world. Agora would have to run its own trial: reset JEFF mid-story, then compare a graph-seeded briefing, a recency briefing and a full-log search by whether each gets him to the right next action, using grounded checks rather than an LLM judge.

## Two hundred neighbours need governance and privacy more than inference

OpenStreetMap shows that a free vocabulary can work at planet scale, and it shows where the governance actually sits. It has three element types (node, way, relation, where relation members carry *roles*), a free `key=value` tag layer, a version check on every edit and full history ([OSM wiki: Elements](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Elements)). Tag proposals need **at least 8 approve votes and at least 75% approval**, yet approval is "non-binding" on mappers and developers, and an approved tag "will not be automatically rendered or added to presets" ([OSM wiki: Proposal process](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Proposal_process)). By 2017 Taginfo counted **more than 89 million distinct tags** ([Mocnik et al.](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/the-openstreetmap-folksonomy-and-its-evolution/)). Critics argued that whoever controls the editor presets steers how people tag ([tagging list 2018](https://lists.openstreetmap.org/pipermail/tagging/2018-June/037401.html)). For Agora, the equivalent of presets is the kind-and-lens picker people see when they add a thing. Whoever curates that list governs the vocabulary, so it should be a visible, forkable world object with its own history and usage counts, not code (imagined). OSM also shows what incentives do to a shared map. Pokémon Go players added fictional parks and footpaths for in-game advantage. One mapper's anecdotal estimate was that about 15% of parks in his area were fake ([talk-us list](https://lists.openstreetmap.org/pipermail/talk-us/2017-January/017044.html)). If Seabright's map ever drives rewards such as fog-of-war unlocks, those rewards have to stay apart from the shared factual layer, or a second neighbour has to confirm any reward-bearing feature (imagined).

Wikidata's governance studies say what disputes will be about. In one sample, **30% of threads were controversial, 62% of controversial threads ended without consensus, and 95% of them concerned modelling practice while only 20% concerned factual accuracy**. Creating a property was much faster than deleting one ([arXiv 2306.11766](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.11766v3)). In a neighbourhood, modelling disputes should be absorbed by stewards and agents and kept out of neighbours' sight. Fact disputes, such as whether a bench is still there, are the ones neighbours can settle (imagined). Wikidata's notability rule also lets an item exist when it "fulfills a structural need," meaning other items need it ([Wikidata: Items](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Wikidata:Items)). That is a graph-native admission rule for a place where nothing is notable to the outside world. A street, a recurring event or a group earns its place because other things refer to it.

The durable civic graphs are narrow. Open Referral's HSDS is a shared vocabulary for one domain, services (organisation, service, location, schedule), with **profiles** that define local variants as changes to a base schema ([HSDS profiles](https://openreferral.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_sources/hsds/profiles.md.txt)). That gives Seabright a ready-made kind set for groups and events, plus the per-community-delta mechanism Agora needs anyway (imagined). The engine of a civic graph is closing the loop. A World Bank-linked analysis of about 400,000 FixMyStreet reports found that people whose first report was fixed were **54% more likely to report again** ([mySociety](https://www.mysociety.org/2014/12/08/research-on-the-impact-of-mysocietys-digital-tools/)). mySociety also refuses to let its data rank areas against each other ([mySociety statement](https://www.mysociety.org/2024/02/06/statement-from-mysociety-regarding-misuse-of-fixmystreet-data/)), which is a reminder that Agora has to decide which community aggregates should exist at all.

The strongest protections in local platforms are structural. Front Porch Forum has human staff review every post before it goes out in a daily digest, and only real-name members may post ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2024/12/05/business/front-porch-forum-social-media-network-vermont-williamstown)). That model scales *down* to a few hundred people, with a rotating steward and a visible reason on every decline (imagined). Nextdoor redesigned its crime-report form to ask for specific descriptive detail. It reported a **75% reduction in racially profiling posts and about 50% more abandoned posts**, and that friction was the point ([Nextdoor blog](https://blog.nextdoor.com/2016/08/24/reducing-racial-profiling-on-nextdoor)). A later investigation found profiling still prevalent ([Wikipedia: Nextdoor](https://Www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nextdoor)). In Agora, the lever is the kind: a "safety report" kind with required fields shapes the input instead of only moderating the output. Location is the most dangerous datum. **Four spatio-temporal points uniquely identify 95% of 1.5 million people**, and coarsening the data buys little anonymity ([de Montjoye et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3607247)). Strava's heatmap exposed military bases because users "in areas without other activity density" stood out ([Engadget via Yahoo](https://sg.finance.yahoo.com/news/exposing-secret-military-bases-strava-194900382.html)). Snap Map's Ghost Mode still placed public posts on the map ([NNEDV Safety Net](https://www.techsafety.org/blog/2017/7/6/whats-the-deal-with-snap-map)). With 200 people, low density is the *default* condition. Exploration traces should stay private to the explorer, consistent with Agora's rule that visual state stays out of the world, and **visibility must be an aspect of each geotagged thing**, not a global user setting (imagined).

For a forkable vocabulary, AT Protocol's lexicon rules are close to a ready-made specification. New fields must be optional, types cannot change, fields cannot be renamed, unknown fields are ignored, and a breaking change takes a new name ([atproto Lexicon spec](https://atproto.com/specs/lexicon)). Bluesky's "stackable moderation" lets users add their own labelers on top of a base layer ([Bluesky blog](https://bsky.social/about/blog/03-12-2024-stackable-moderation)), which maps onto each neighbour's personal agent subscribing to community and personal filters (imagined). DNS-rooted authority does not fit a neighbourhood. Agora's root would be the community's own world or charter, with a path for promoting a person's private kinds into the community namespace, like Freebase's promotion rule. The Niantic history shows what happens without shared ids. Community-nominated points outlived the games built on them, but when the games changed hands the Wayfarer and Ingress databases split with no merge path ([Wayfarer community](https://community.wayfarer.nianticlabs.com/t/wayfarer-and-ingress-updates-discussion/83723?page=2)). Agora's forks should keep shared ids and provenance so reconciliation stays possible.

## Graphs give what folders cannot: many paths in, reasons attached

Bush's complaint about folders was that an item lives in one place and is reached by one path ([Bush 1945](https://web.mit.edu/STS.035/www/PDFs/think.pdf)). Graphs give each thing many ways in, and every derived suggestion can carry its derivation. Faceted browsing over typed properties has the most consistent user evidence. In the Flamenco study, **90% of 32 participants preferred the faceted interface** over a standard image search across 35,000 images, and 97% said it helped them learn the collection ([Yee et al., CHI 2003](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse454/13wi/papers/yee-chi03.pdf)). Whole-graph pictures have measured limits. People had significant difficulty finding shortest paths above **about 50 nodes in dense node-link diagrams and about 100 in sparse ones** ([Yoghourdjian et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.07944)). So the graph should drive facets (each kind a facet, each aspect key a facet, each relation a pivot), ego-centred neighbourhood lists, and computed answers to "how is A connected to B" that show a few paths. A whole-world picture belongs only in a diagnostic lens (imagined). That also fits Alex's preference for stable layouts over animated physics.

Recommendation and link suggestion are cheap and explainable at Agora's scale. Pinterest's Pixie runs random walks with restart on a graph of **3 billion nodes and 17 billion edges** and reported **up to 50% higher engagement** than the previous system. Pruning hub nodes gave a further **58% quality improvement**, and one server answers 1,200 requests a second at 60 ms ([Eksombatchai et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.07601.pdf)). A careful RecSys reproduction could reproduce only **7 of 18** neural recommenders, and most could be beaten on some datasets by simpler methods ([Ferrari Dacrema et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1907.06902)). Link prediction from topology alone works well above chance. A secondary review of Liben-Nowell and Kleinberg reports Adamic–Adar at **54.4× random** on one co-authorship network ([Liben-Nowell & Kleinberg](https://people.csail.mit.edu/dln/papers/link/paper.pdf); [secondary review](https://liner.com/review/link-prediction-problem-for-social-networks)). A NeurIPS 2023 benchmark found that reported GNN gains partly reflect weak baselines and negatives that are too easy to reject ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10453v1)). Two hundred neighbours is a cold-start regime where collaborative filtering struggles. Walks that also traverse author, place, kind and topic edges, with degree penalties so a busy board doesn't dominate, should outperform it (imagined; no community-scale deployment study was found).

Identity needs the most care. The proven entity-resolution pipeline has three parts: blocking to avoid quadratic comparison ([Papadakis et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06167v3)), Fellegi–Sunter's two thresholds with a human-review middle band, which cut review volume by at least 3× in one study ([ISI paper](https://isi-web.org/sites/default/files/import/files-2011/450070.pdf)), and review that does not treat human labels as error-free ([arXiv 2412.04178](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04178)). Given the sameAs history, a merge in Agora should be a reversible, attributed event ("A same-as B, by whom, on what evidence"). A view resolves the cluster, an unmerge is another event, and weaker relations such as "same place as" never copy properties across owners (imagined). For emergent groups, the algorithm matters too. Louvain produced badly connected communities up to **25%** of the time and disconnected ones up to **16%**, while Leiden guarantees connected communities ([Traag et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.08473)). Clusters should be shown as named, dated snapshots, not stable identities. Explanations are what graphs give for free, but their effect should be stated honestly. Explanations raise *acceptance* of recommendations ([Herlocker et al.](https://typeset.io/pdf/explaining-collaborative-filtering-recommendations-17bchfs9p8.pdf)), while a meta-analysis of 97 XAI studies found their effects on *trust* inconsistent ([arXiv 2509.18980](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.18980v1)). Agora should aim explanations at scrutability, so a person can strike out "not because of Sam" and re-rank, rather than at persuasion.

None of this needs big infrastructure. At 10³–10⁵ things, everything above except GNN training and full concept lattices plausibly runs in memory on one machine. That is an estimate from recall, *not run*, and a small benchmark on a synthetic Agora-shaped graph in `docs/design/kinds/` should come before anyone relies on it. The engineering problem is incremental maintenance from the log. Adjacency and facet counts update on each event. Local scores are computed on demand. Global analytics run on a slow cadence and are stamped with the log position they reflect. The table below ranks the mechanisms by value for cost at Agora's scale (imagined synthesis of the evidence above).

| Rank | Mechanism | Evidence it rests on | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Backlinks, history and as-of readings derived from the log | Bush, Datomic, PKM user reports | Hub things link to everything |
| 2 | Statement shape: provenance, rank, supersession; retract vs excise | Wikidata, Datomic, Graphiti | Rank inflation by agents |
| 3 | Verbs bound to aspects, per-agent grants with a confirm flag | Palantir, Windows shell, ECS, Tana's API caveat | Non-local behaviour, needs verb provenance |

## Conclusion

The history reframes Agora's question. Every system that built a knowledge graph had to bolt on what Agora started with: provenance on each fact, a log that is never overwritten, actors acting on behalf of principals, and a record of what the decision-maker saw. Agora's kernel already has those, so the open design problem has moved to the *readings*. Which derived views exist, how agents walk them, and how proposals become facts. The literature's failures cluster at two edges Agora is pulled toward: up-front ontologies that ask people to classify before they get anything back, and machine-extracted graphs whose cost and noise exceed their gains. Agora's authored edges and append-only shape put it between those two, which is the most promising position. It is also an untested one, because no published benchmark covers a multi-writer world of people and agents. The one rule that ties every successful mechanism together, from Wikidata's Primary Sources queue and Palantir's confirm flag to Fellegi–Sunter's review band and FIDES's escalation, is to make the machine's contribution a reasoned, reversible proposal and to let a person or the owning agent accept it.

Two things would change this assessment. If Agora's authored edges turn out too sparse to connect what matters, LLM extraction and its costs come back onto the table, and the GraphRAG and HippoRAG evidence becomes directly relevant. If a JEFF context-reset trial shows that a recency briefing over the log does as well as a graph-seeded one, the graph's case for agent memory shrinks to temporal validity alone. The cheapest next step is to run both checks in the kinds lab: a synthetic benchmark of graph readings at 10⁵ things, and a reset trial scored on grounded next actions rather than by an LLM judge.
