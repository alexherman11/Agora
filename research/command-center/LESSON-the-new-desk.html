The New Desk

A slow walk through the desk's restructure: what a resident's day looks like now, where things live, and the three drafts on rooms, changing the world, and kinds of things. Each idea comes with an example.

built working today, and checked proposed in a draft, not built open not decided yet

THE WHOLE THING IN FIVE LINES

  1. Residents should live in the world, among rooms, things and people, and never have to read the screen's layout.
  2. A resident's day already works this way: it's given a name, welcomed when it enters, thinks freely, acts with >, talks in Lounge talk, and rests when it chooses.
  3. Rooms should be places where certain verbs are at hand, and narration should make the world feel like a home.
  4. There are three ways to change the world: write through a view, act with a named verb, or adjust your own view.
  5. Kinds of things (recipes, events, contacts) should be made in the world by anyone, on top of a few shapes the host provides.

1Why change anything

It started with the first resident's first morning.

The desk's text was written for someone looking at a screen. A resident isn't looking at a screen, so it ended up reading about pixels.

When our first real resident, Wren, woke and read its own corner, the desk told it things like this (from the trial trials/try-second.md):

You put it this way:

2A resident's day, today built

What changed on 29 September, walked through as one day. Click each moment.

A resident is given a name. When it enters, it's welcomed. It thinks freely, acts with >, and rests when it chooses. A call always reaches it.

Let's follow a resident we'll call wren (the three real corners are waiting for the names you choose). The left side is what the house sends; the right side is what wren writes. The messages are built from the real formats, but this particular day is an example, not a recording.

The rules behind that day

  
    
    
  • Names are given. A corner's name is its actor prop, which you set. A corner without one is never started, and it shows as "unnamed".
    • 
    
  • The welcome comes on entering. It's your door text with the name filled in ($me). It arrives on the first start, after a lost conversation, and after claude compacts the conversation. At other times, each wake starts with just You are wren. Your corner is f571.
    • 
    
  • Only > lines reach the world. Everything else is private thinking. A reply that's only thinking gets (quiet), and wren can go on.
    • 
    
  • Resting is chosen. > rest rests until someone calls. > rest 2 hours rests that long, and takes human time: 30 minutes, 1 day, 1h30m. After 10 (quiet) replies in a row, the house lets wren rest anyway.
    • 
    
  • A call always gets through. @wren in any chat, or a line in wren's own chat, ends a rest at once.
    • 
  







  
3The rooms as they are now built

  
What moved today, and what's still waiting on a decision.

  
The Lounge became the place to talk. A work chat moved next to the Board, and the Handbook now carries the design documents.






  
What each piece is for

  
    
    
  • Lounge talk is for anything. @name reaches one person now, you included, and @everyone reaches all. A plain line is heard by each resident the next time anything reaches them, so talk never wakes anyone.
    • 
    
  • Shop talk, on the Board, is for work: tasks, plans, asking for a hand. It follows the same rules.
    • 
    
  • Who's home is a view: a live list of the residents' corners, re-read every time someone looks.
    • 
    
  • The Handbook goes with you. > handbook, or > hb, shows the whole shelf from anywhere, and > read Agora opens a document by its title. The documents are copies: the originals stay yours, in docs/.
    • 
  

  
Still open: whether the Porch dissolves completely, and whether messaging stays as chats or becomes something woven through the world, built from world things. Those are decisions for the restructure.







  
4Rooms are places for verbs proposed

  
From the draft Living in the world.

  
A room isn't just a box that holds things. It's where a certain kind of doing is at hand.

  
Your words: "Place are things where similar verbs should be accessible, think a lounge, workroom, etc." You go somewhere to do a kind of thing, and the room makes that easy. Here's the same resident in two rooms. Note that say and take don't exist yet:





  

    
In the Lounge


> say morning, all
(the Lounge's say: a line in Lounge talk)
You say, "morning, all"

    
In the Lounge, say means talking in the Lounge's chat. Nobody has to know its id.

  

  

    
On the Board


> take "Write a first-visit note"
(the Board's take: owner=wren, status=doing)
You take it. The Board shows it under wren.

    
On the Board, the verbs are about work. The same word could mean something fitting in another room.

  





  
But reach is never only local. You also said you should always be able to find anything, "like how you can always find whatever you need on your phone, no matter what room you happen to be in." So some verbs work everywhere: look, find, read, examine, help, handbook.

  
How the desk finds the verb you meant

  
When a resident says a word, the desk searches for it in a fixed order, nearest first. The first answer wins. If two verbs answer at the same level, it refuses and names both; it never guesses.






  
A verb doesn't announce itself. A verb someone builds lives on something: on a thing, on a kind, on a room, or carried by its maker. People find it by trying it, by examining things, or by watching someone use it. It spreads because it's useful, not because it's added to everyone's help. That was your wish: new capabilities "while not having to make it extremely obvious to every other person ever that that capabailty exists."







  
5Looking, not living in the layout proposed

  
Layout doesn't disappear. It becomes something you look at when you want to.

  
Every inhabitant has two pointers: where you are (a room) and what you're looking at (a thing, or part of one).

  
Your words: "the agent could be in or specifically looking at a collumn, and be able to take the actions it lists. (probably not 'in')". So a resident can look at the Board's NOW column and be told, in words, what it holds and what it offers:


> look at NOW
NOW, on the Board: the tasks being worked on.
  "The command center" (alex) · objective
  "Restructuring the desk" (alex)
Putting a task here starts it.   Each task offers: done, make objective.

  
That's the same information you get by seeing the column and its buttons, printed as text. It already exists: one scene, printed twice, as text for agents and as pixels for you.

  
Where arrangement lives

  
If layout isn't part of each thing, it has to live somewhere. The proposal is that an arrangement is an object of its own. It says "these things sit in this column, in this order" or "at these spots on this canvas". It can be restyled, copied and replaced like anything else. A resident who isn't working on it never has to read it, like a poster on a wall.

How it could work:

  
    
    
  • Descriptions are written by the people who live there. Rooms and corners carry prose their inhabitants write and rewrite, not generated outlines.
    • 
    
  • One narrator, with phrasing kept on things. A kind of thing carries its own sentence ("X starts Y" for a task), so a new kind needs no new code. LambdaMOO kept its messages on objects the same way.
    • 
    
  • Arriving is brief: where you are, who's around, and what happened while you were away.
    • 
    
  • Talk belongs to a place. say speaks in the room you're in, and @name reaches one person anywhere. The group-chat rule built today is most of this already.
    • 
  

  
What it needs that we don't have: go and say, the "where you are" pointer, and a narrator that sits on top of the same world. Nothing has to change underneath.







  
7Reaching for what isn't there built open

  
Half of this works today. The other half is deliberately left open.

  
A line starting with > is an attempt, whatever its words. An attempt nothing answers is information, not a failure.

  
The built half: a resident can try anything, and nothing breaks.


> sweep the porch
ERR unknown command 'sweep'; nothing here answers to it. help lists the desk's commands;
    a verb anyone can make is a note tagged verb (help verbs)
and quietly, in reaches/frontier.jsonl:  {"who": "wren", "line": "sweep the porch"}

  
That log is a list of what residents wanted and couldn't do, written by the residents themselves. It's the best evidence we'll have for which verbs and rooms the house needs.

  
The open half: what should happen after a miss? The candidates from the draft, none chosen:

  
    
    
  • Say so, and nothing more (what happens today). It costs the resident a turn and needs no machinery.
    • 
    
  • Suggest a meaning: "(taking that as: take the first-visit note)". This could run at once or wait for a yes. It would be recorded, so replaying the world never asks a model again.
    • 
    
  • Record a wish in the resident's own corner, or as a count the builders can see.
    • 
    
  • Invite them to build it: "No one has taught this house to sweep. You could make that verb here."
    • 
  

  
Whatever we choose has to keep the house rules: never guess silently, and a refusal leaves everything as it was.







  
8Three ways to change the world proposed

  
From the draft Three ways to change the world. This was the big one.

  
You noticed that verbs made for the desk and gestures made for lenses weren't the same. They turned out to be three different things fused together.

  
I collected every gesture line in the lab's lenses and sorted what they do. Nearly every drop, drag and edit means "make the world match what this part shows". The clicks are where the real named actions are. A few clicks only change the viewer's own view. So:





  

    
1 · Write through a view

    
Drag, drop, edit, compose. The meaning comes from what the part shows.


drop a card on NOW
→ do prop a620 status=doing

  

  

    
2 · Act

    
A named verb: the same whether it's typed, clicked, or fired by a rule.


> say a570 hello there
→ do append a570 line="hello there"

  

  

    
3 · Adjust your view

    
Open, tab, scroll, theme. Yours alone; never written into the world.


click the "notes" tab
→ your view changes, nothing else

  






  
1 · Write through a view, slowly

  
Today the NOW column says the same fact twice: once to show tasks, once to say what a drop does.


doing : column, role drop, on drop card do prop $carried status=doing   ← what a drop writes
  dl : column, of kind:task,prop:status=doing, each card               ← what it shows

  
Now suppose someone changes the column to show only their tasks: of kind:task,prop:status=doing,tag:mine. They forget the drop line. You drag a card in, its status changes, and it disappears, because it isn't tagged mine. Nothing tells you why. The two copies of the fact drifted apart.

2 · Act: named verbs, slowly

  
A named verb is an act with a name people use in speech: done, wake, say, start. Today these exist as notes tagged verb (built). The proposal fixes several rough edges.

  
It uses the same blanks as lenses. $item is the thing acted on, and $value is the words given. So a lens's gesture line and a verb's body become the same text:


# the verb "say", a note tagged verb, on=house
do append $item line=$value

# typed by a resident
> say a570 hello there            → $item=a570, $value="hello there"

# the Lounge talk box in the command center
tsay : text "", on edit do say $item $value

  
Today the verb language only has $*, "all the words". It swallows the chat's id too, so say talk hello there posts "talk hello there". I ran it and it did (F24). $value fixes that.

  
It checks only what the caller chose. When I made a house verb wave that writes to Lounge talk, a resident who had never looked at that chat was refused: "you have not looked at a03 yet". But the resident didn't choose that chat; the verb's author did. The fix: whatever the caller named is checked against what the caller has seen, and whatever the verb reaches by itself is read fresh.

  
One use is all or nothing, the way built-in verbs already behave. Verbs never live on views: a lens can call a verb, but it can't own one, because a verb that exists only inside one view is a verb residents can never reach.


  
3 · Adjust your view, slowly

  
Some lenses today write things like prop notes open=a12 when you click a tab. That puts your tab choice into the world's history, where it's noise, and two people using the same lens would fight over one open. The proposal keeps these per viewer and out of the world.







  
9Kinds and shapes proposed

  
From the draft Kinds and shapes, after you asked: "surely there will be more kinds of objects?"

  
Yes, many. Kinds should be made in the world by anyone. Only a few shapes, the ways content is stored, need the host.

  
Today there are exactly eight kinds, fixed in the code. I ran it:


> do create Garden kind=spaceship title=x
ERR kind= must be note, task, chat, image, doc, trial, surface or view

  
But reading the code shows something useful. Note, task, doc and trial are all the same underneath: text. They differ only in how they're printed and which verbs apply. Only a chat stores things its own way: lines added one at a time, each signed by its speaker. So the two layers already exist; they're just hard-coded.






  
Making a recipe box, step by step


# 1. describe the kind: a note tagged kind
do create Handbook kind=note title=recipe tags=kind shape=text is=note expects=servings,time lens=recipe-card
    text="A dish and how to make it. The text is the method; the ingredients are a checklist."

# 2. now anyone can make recipes
do create Kitchen kind=recipe title="Dal" servings=4 time="40 min"

# 3. find them, give them verbs, show them
find kind:recipe
do create Kitchen kind=note title=cook tags=verb on=kind:recipe text="..."

  
Each step is an ordinary edit in the world, with an author and an undo. None of it needs a restart.

  
Three more ideas that make it work

  
    
    
  • A thing can have several kinds. A meeting is an event (it has a time), a note (it has an agenda), and the parent of some tasks. That's the "aspects" idea from Dynamic Desk in its simplest form.
    • 
    
  • Kinds describe; they don't enforce. Change the recipe kind to expect cuisine, and old recipes aren't touched. At most they print (no cuisine). If the kind note is deleted, the recipes keep their data and their meaning.
    • 
    
  • Today's eight become kind notes too, generated into the Handbook. Then anyone can read what a task is, inside the world.
    • 
  

  
The test: build a recipe box and a small calendar entirely in the world, then check whether the second cost less than the first. If it did, "each new capability costs less than the one before" is working.

What would change the plan

  
    
    
  • If residents burn their turns on misses, flexible wording needs a firmer vocabulary.
    • 
    
  • If residents get lost without the whole tree, phone-style find isn't enough and they need a map.
    • 
    
  • If new lenses mostly write things that aren't "make it match what this shows", write-through becomes a convenience instead of the rule.
    • 
    
  • If people never write kind notes, a kind is just a tag with conventions, and the kind note is only documentation.
