How the Desk Grows

A walkthrough of what was built on 28 and 29 September: verbs anyone can make in the world, workers that change the desk's code, and the keeper that keeps it running. Then how these sit beside the lens compiler, and how far we are from one substrate.

THE SHORT VERSION

  1. Most changes stay in the world. Notes, lenses, styles and now verbs are all things in the world. You change them with a desk line: the change has an author, can be undone, and is live at once. That is tier 1.
  2. Some changes need new host code. For those, you commission a worker. It works in its own git worktree, talks to you through comments on the task, and the house merges its work only if health checks pass. That is tier 2.
  3. The keeper is the small fixed part outside both. It restarts things on new code, and undoes a landing that keeps crashing the house.
  4. Lenses and verbs are two halves of one idea: a lens decides what you see, and a verb decides what you can do. Both are text in a note. The lens goes through a real compiler. The verb, for now, is filled in like a template and run line by line.
  5. How close to one substrate: close for what a person makes, and still far for what the desk itself is made of.

1Three depths of change

Everything here comes down to one question: how deep does a change have to go?

Anything that changes the desk sits at one of three depths. Tier 1 made the middle depth bigger, and tier 2 opened a way down to the bottom one from inside the world.

Before these two changes, making a new verb meant going below the line: add an @verb to moo.py, then restart the server. The other option was script, which is a setting on the desk rather than a thing in the world. It has no author and no history, and you can't undo it. So the most common way someone would want to extend the desk was also the most expensive way to do it.

2One line, one event

The foundation every change rests on, including verbs.

Everything that changes the world arrives as a desk line: from the terminal, from a resident, from a click in the painter, or from a verb. Each line goes through the same four steps.

Keep this picture in mind for the rest of the lesson. A verb doesn't add a new kind of change. It produces ordinary lines, and each of them goes through this same path.

3Seeing: the lens compiler

A lens is a note that says how part of the world is shown. The staged compiler turns it into a scene.

Here is part of the real command-center lens, a589, the one on your screen now:

# the Board's NOW column (from research/command-center/seed/command-center.lens)
doing : column, role drop, on drop card do prop $carried status=doing
  dh : text "NOW", role meta
  dl : column, of kind:task,prop:status=doing, each card

In of kind:task,prop:status=doing, the lens reads the world with a selection. In on drop card do prop $carried status=doing, the lens says what a gesture writes back, and it says it as a desk line. That second half is where verbs will come in.

Most of the machinery in a lens is on the reading side: selections, frames, derives, guards. The writing side is thin, because an acts node holds a desk line and nothing more. That is why verbs fit so naturally. They let that desk line say something bigger than one primitive.

4Doing: verbs in the world

Tier 1. A verb is a note tagged verb. Its title is the word, and its text is desk lines.

Making one

# wren makes a verb. It is a note, so this is one ordinary create event.
do create Home kind=note title="wave" tags=verb text="# wave at someone in Porch talk\ndo append a570 line=\"*$me waves at $1*\""

# wren tries it. For now wren is the only one who can use it: a verb starts out carried by its maker.
wave thimble
(wave a601: do append a570 line="*wren waves at thimble*")
ok append a570 ...

# wren puts it on the house, so everyone can use it
do prop a601 on=house

The blanks: $1 $2 … are the words after the verb, $* is all of them at once, $1.title is the title of the thing $1 names, $me is whoever is using it, and $this is the thing the verb lives on. Lines starting with # are its description, which help wave shows.

Where a verb lives decides who can use it

The prop on is the verb's place:

  • No on: carried by its maker, a private draft.
  • on=a589: sits on a thing, so anyone who names that thing can use it.
  • on=house: everyone, everywhere.

This is the MOO idea you pointed at: the verbs you can use depend on what you carry and what you're near. Lookup goes innermost first, and the first level with an answer wins. If two verbs answer at the same level, the desk refuses and names both. It never picks one for you.

What happens to a line nothing else claims

Running a verb: a macro that stays honest

When a verb answers, verbs.run fills in its blanks, joins its lines with ; into one batch, and sends that batch through the desk as you, never as the verb's author. The steps run in order and stop at the first refusal. The receipt says which step failed. undo takes back the whole use.

There was one choice to make here. How should the events a verb produces remember that the verb made them?

Rejected: record the verb as the event's own verb

{verb: "wave", target: a570, via: "wave"}

The fence knows which aspects a verb touches by its name. append touches text; wave is unknown. An unknown verb is treated as touching everything, so every use would be refused as soon as anyone had touched the chat.

Chosen: the step keeps its own verb; provenance goes beside it

{verb: "append", target: a570,
 verb_run: "wave a601"}

The fence judges append exactly as it would if you had typed it. history shows (verb wave a601), so you can still see where the change came from. Checked in a throwaway world ran.

A snag found while writing this lesson

Your residents' first complaint (F21) was that there is no say. A verb should fix that, so I tried it in a scratch world ran:

do create desk kind=note title=say tags=verb on=house text="do append $1 line=$*"
say talk hello there
(say a05: do append talk line="talk hello there")   ← $* includes the chat's name

# what works today: quote the words and use $2
say2 talk "hello there"
(say2 a07: do append talk line="hello there")

$* means every word, including the target. The verb language has no "the rest after $1." That is a missing piece of tier 1 that only tier 2 can add, because it lives in verbs.py. It's also a perfect small commission. I've logged it as friction F24.

5Verbs beside the compiler

You asked how the new verbs relate to the staged compiler. They are its missing twin, built far more simply.

A lens and a verb are the same kind of thing: text in a note that the desk interprets, live, with no restart. One reads the world, the other writes to it. Lined up stage by stage, the lens goes through a real compiler, and the verb goes through a template and a batch.

StageLens (seeing)Verb (doing)
CheckedAt every build, by the parser's word table. A broken lens keeps showing its last good version and says so first.Only when it is used. A broken verb fails at its step, and nothing checks it when you save it.
Names resolvedScope, innermost first: lets, frames, the thing, then the host (N1–N6)Place, innermost first: what you carry, the thing named, then the house
Lowered toSix IR kinds: bind, lets, guard, emit, acts, placeNothing. Text substitution into desk lines.
Runs throughevaluate: a pure function, reading only through the Accessor, with every read loggeddispatch, as the invoker; the fence judges each step as it runs (what it reads isn't logged)
Leaves behindA scene and no events, printed two waysEvents tagged verb_run, a receipt, one undo
Blanks$title $item $name.field, derive$1 $* $me $this $1.title, a second dialect

Two rows line up in a way that isn't an accident. Scope for lens names and place for verbs follow the same rule: look innermost first, and refuse if something is ambiguous. And the lens's acts node already produces a desk line, which is exactly what a verb takes in.

Where they meet today

A lens gesture sends its desk line in the do form, and the do form reaches world verbs. I ran that path in the scratch world (do say2 talk "via do" posted). A lens could therefore say:

tsay : text "", hint "say something", on edit do say $item $value
doing : column, role drop, on drop card do start $carried

Here start would be one verb, written once, used by the Board's drag, by your terminal, and by a resident typing start a593. That is the Held claim "verbs learned once, used anywhere," reached through the world instead of through Python. read the gesture path in deskd.py; I didn't click a painted gesture that calls a verb.

Where they should meet imagined

My proposal, to argue with rather than build yet:

  • Check a verb when it is saved, as a lens is compiled. Parse its lines against the desk's own grammar, and refuse an unknown command or a $3 the verb can't fill, before anyone is surprised by it.
  • Lower a verb into the acts kind. A verb becomes a named, reusable act, which a lens can call the same way a lens places another lens.
  • One set of blanks (decision 2 in the round-six proposition). $1 and $title become the same expression language used in two places, and $rest falls out of it.
  • Reads through the Accessor. A verb's use would carry a read-set like a painted gesture does, so "stale" would name exactly what the verb looked at.

What would change my mind: if residents with a real model write verbs that are mostly one line, then the macro is the right weight and a compiler stage would be ceremony. The reaches log and the verbs they write will tell us.

6When the world can't say it: commissions

Tier 2. Some changes are below the line: a new primitive, a new lens word, a fix to the painter.

The world can still ask. A task with commission=asked says what, why, and how you'll know it's done. You decided commissions are trusted, not granted by you. The asker only needs a channel back, and that channel is comments on the task.

Landing: health on both sides of the merge

What has actually happened ran: two real workers went from asked to landed on trial branches. One fixed help listing verbs the desk couldn't do. 7The keeper: the way back

If a landing can break the house, something outside the house has to be able to undo it.

Dynamic Desk leans toward: "There is always a way back when something breaks. Something independent can restore and recover." keeper.py is that something. It is 118 lines, meant to change rarely, and it depends on none of the code it protects.

Tested against itself ran in a throwaway checkout. A new commit restarted deskd, and the version changed. A killed house came back after 30 s. A deliberately broken land commit was reverted after three deaths. The test also found three bugs, now fixed (F23):

  • it counted crashes from before the landing against it;
  • a hard-killed keeper left an orphan house running, which the one-house rule (exit 76) now guards against;
  • deskd on a taken port would have restarted every 2 seconds.

Not yet: the keeper running on frontier itself, or a real landing under the keeper that ends in exit 75.

8Two histories

You asked whether worktrees should use native versioning or git. The answer we built: both, each for what it is good at, joined by the commission.

Why two. World events are made for things. They have one author each, work at the grain of a single aspect, and are checked by the fence and undone one by one. Code needs line diffs, branches, three-way merges, and the tools every worker already knows. The world's own worktree merge replays command lines all or nothing, which is right for a world and wrong for a Python file. So the world is versioned natively and the host code in git read.

The cost: two undo stories. undo eN can't take back a landing; git revert and the keeper do. If the host code ever becomes things in the world, git would become the way those things are stored and moved, not a second history. That is the long horizon, not the next step imagined.

9How the world gets built

Putting it together: the rungs, from cheapest to most expensive. Go as shallow as the change allows.

  1. 0ReachTry it. If nothing answers, it's logged.> tidy the porch · reaches/
  2. 1ContentChange the things themselvesdo append · do prop · undo eN
  3. 2ViewA lens note: what is shown, and what gestures writerewrite a589 · repaints live
  4. 3LookA style note: tokens and rolesrewrite a587 (pixel)
  5. 4VerbA note tagged verb: what anyone can docarried → on=house · live at once
  6. 5ReactionA rule or script: happens by itself. Not yet a thing in the world.rule when … do … · no author, no undo
  7. 6HostA commission: a worker changes the codecommission=asked · lands in minutes

A lens (rung 2) plus verbs (rung 4) plus a style (rung 3) over some notes (rung 1) is an app, and none of it is Python. The command center is already rungs 1–3. With verbs, its gestures can say start or say instead of spelling out primitives.

The rungs also form a loop. That loop is how the desk improves itself.

The verdict

For what a person makes, we're close. Content, views, looks, verbs and the residents themselves are all things in the world: one kind of object, one line language, and one fence, history and undo. A person can now give an app its own verbs without touching Python. Three days ago that took moo.py.

For what the desk is made of, we're still far. Every primitive, every lens word and the painter are host code, and the host is still growing (scene.py is 1,440 lines). The Ingalls standard asks for "a minimum set of unchangeable parts." We have a large set of parts that can be changed, but only from outside the world. Tier 2 builds a door through that wall. It doesn't remove the wall.

The three moves that would close the most distance imagined

  • Rules and scripts become notes, as styles did in F7. Scripts are already mostly covered by verbs, so they could simply retire. Rules become notes tagged rule, with authors and undo. This is small, and it finishes "definitions are uniform."
  • One expression language (decision 2), and verbs through the compiler as acts (chapter 5). Then seeing and doing share one grammar, one scope rule and one read log.
  • Primitives described in the world. Each built-in verb gets a generated note showing its arguments, what it touches and an example, so examine treats them like world verbs. They'd still be implemented in the host, but they'd be visible and addressable in the world. That's the first step toward the host being made of things.

What would change my mind:

  • If live residents keep commissioning things a verb could have done, tier 1 is too hard to find, and the fix is discovery, not more machinery.
  • If verbs pile up with clashing names, lookup should go by identity, not by word.
  • If landings go wrong in ways health can't see, "health before and after" is too weak, and landings need a staging world first.
